

**Coordinates**

In the context of complex numbers and their geometric interpretation, a "Coordinates" section typically covers:

- **Cartesian (rectangular) coordinates**: a complex number z = a + bi is identified with the point (a, b) in the plane, where a is the real part (x-coordinate) and b is the imaginary part (y-coordinate). This is the basis for the "complex plane" or "Argand diagram."

- **The real and imaginary axes**: the horizontal axis represents purely real numbers, the vertical axis represents purely imaginary numbers, and the plane as a whole represents all complex numbers.

- **Polar coordinates**: the same point can be described by (r, θ), where r = |z| is the distance from the origin and θ = arg(z) is the angle from the positive real axis. The connection between the two systems:
$$a = r\cos\theta, \quad b = r\sin\theta$$

- **Coordinate transformations**: converting between rectangular and polar forms, and how operations look different in each — addition is natural in rectangular coordinates, while multiplication (and rotation) is natural in polar coordinates.

- **Extension to three dimensions**: this section may also start previewing spatial coordinate systems (x, y, z axes) as a setup for discussing vectors and rotations in 3D space — since the eventual goal of the book is representing 3D rotations using quaternions, the notion of a coordinate frame and how it transforms under rotation is foundational.

- **Right-handed vs. left-handed coordinate systems**: a detail that matters a great deal in rotation mathematics, since the sign conventions in cross products and rotation formulas depend on this choice — likely introduced here or very soon after.
